Bread as Infrastructure

Most people think sourdough is about bread.

It isn't.

At least not entirely — it’s a lesson in systems engineering.

← Back to xCoreTX

Design

Building and maintaining a sourdough culture taught me many of the same lessons I encounter when designing networks, managing infrastructure, and troubleshooting complex systems: stability takes time, observation matters more than assumptions, and forcing a system rarely produces the desired outcome.

What began as an experiment with einkorn flour quickly became a lesson in systems engineering. Creating a sourdough starter from scratch using einkorn flour is a lesson in managing fragile systems.

Einkorn is an ancient grain. It ferments faster, absorbs less water, and has a weaker gluten structure than modern wheat. Working with einkorn felt less like baking and more like engineering.

Initializing the System

Starting a sourdough culture is similar to provisioning a new server in an unfamiliar environment. You mix flour and water. Wild yeast begins competing with bacteria. For several days, the system looks unstable. It rises. It collapses. It smells questionable. You see activity, but not reliability.

These are false positives. In networking, early deployments often behave the same way. Services start. Logs populate. Traffic moves. But the system is not yet stable.

With einkorn, it took nearly ten days before the starter rose and fell predictably. Just because something boots does not mean it is production-ready.

Maintenance and Monitoring

Once active, the starter requires consistent feeding. Einkorn prefers a thicker ratio — often around 1:0.5 flour to water. Too much hydration and the structure breaks down. Too little attention and it weakens. This is routine maintenance.

Networks require patching, monitoring, and controlled resource allocation. Ignore updates and vulnerabilities accumulate. Overload services and performance degrades. You do not measure health by activity alone.

A starter that doubles in size but smells off is like a server with high CPU and silent errors in the logs. Growth is not the same as stability.

Staging Before Production

When baking, you do not use the main starter directly. You take a portion and build a levain. A Levain is a specifically prepared culture designed for that single bake.

  • The starter is development.
  • The levain is staging
  • The final dough is production.
Every environment serves a different purpose, even though they originate from the same source. You test strength. You confirm timing. You validate structure before introducing it to the full dough. In IT, pushing directly from development to production is reckless. In baking, using the entire starter without building a levain carries similar risk. Same culture. Different preparation. Different environment.

Change Management

Einkorn dough does not tolerate aggressive kneading. Its gluten network is delicate. Instead of force, it requires gentle folds and precise timing.

This mirrors configuration changes in networking. You cannot brute-force adjustments into a fragile system. Overworking dough leads to collapse. Overloading infrastructure leads to outages. Small changes. Measured intervals. Continuous observation. Stability is built gradually.

Troubleshooting the Boule

When the final loaf comes out flat or dense, frustration does not solve the problem. Scope does.

  • Was the starter weak?
  • Was fermentation too long?
  • Was hydration miscalculated?
  • Was the environment too warm?
In networking, the questions are similar:
  • Was it misconfiguration?
  • Resource exhaustion?
  • Environmental variables?
  • Physical layer failure?
Both require reviewing the logs. For sourdough, the logs are time, temperature, hydration, texture, and smell. For networks, they are metrics, traces, and system output. The process is the same: isolate variables, test assumptions, adjust.

Documentation Matters

Experienced bakers keep notes.

  • Hydration percentages.
  • Temperatures.
  • Fermentation times.
  • Flour blends.
Without documentation, every loaf becomes a new experiment. Infrastructure behaves the same way. Good documentation transforms troubleshooting from guesswork into analysis. Memory is unreliable. Records are infrastructure.

Invisible Progress

Sourdough is often described as fifteen minutes of work spread across three days. Most of the transformation happens quietly.

IT careers follow a similar pattern. Study happens privately. Labs fail silently. Migrations require hours of waiting for minutes of execution. Progress compounds beneath the surface.

Final Thought

Whether building networks or bread, stability is earned — not assumed.

This isn’t about baking bread. It’s about understanding systems.

Stability is not created by force. It is built through discipline.

Learn. Apply. Grow.
